you> Explain the model swap experiment.
retrieval_decision=True top_bm25=5.926193072240241
wiki> The model swap experiment is a diagnostic technique used to determine whether performance bottlenecks lie with the AI model itself or with the surrounding execution environment (the Harness). [S1]

**Purpose and Method**
The goal is to isolate the model's contribution to the overall performance. This is achieved by:

1.  Keeping the Harness (which includes prompts, tool design, and feedback loops) completely constant.
2.  Swapping in a stronger or weaker model.
3.  Observing how much the score moves.

**Interpreting the Results**
The experiment helps distinguish between two potential issues:

*   **If a stronger model does not raise the score:** The bottleneck is likely the Harness design. [S1]
*   **If a weaker model tanks the score, and results swing sharply with the model's capability:** The model itself is the bottleneck. [S1]

**Distinction from Ablation**
It is important to note that this differs from an ablation experiment. While ablation involves disabling a Harness component to see how performance changes, model swapping holds the Harness constant and only changes the model to determine the source of the performance variance. [S1]

you> What is pass^k?
retrieval_decision=True top_bm25=2.717970948534941
wiki> $\text{Pass}^k$ is a metric used to measure the **reliability** of an AI Agent, distinct from its raw capability.

Here is a breakdown of what it means:

1.  **Operational Definition:** $\text{Pass}^k$ requires running the exact same task $k$ times in a row, and demanding that *every single run* succeeds without triggering any automatic failure criteria (like safety violations or hallucinations) [S1].
2.  **Probabilistic Relationship:** If $p$ is the single-run success rate, the probability of achieving $\text{Pass}^k$ is $p^k$ [S1].

**Key Distinction:**

*   **$\text{Pass@k}$** measures the upper bound of capability—it requires that *at least one* of $k$ runs succeeds [S1, S4].
*   **$\text{Pass}^k$** measures the stability and reliability—it requires *all* $k$ runs to succeed [S1, S3].

For critical operations, $\text{Pass}^k$ is the appropriate metric, as it reflects the stability of "never making a mistake" [S3].
